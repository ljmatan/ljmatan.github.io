Usage guide

When the person isn't interested yet

The question caretakers ask most often: "They don't seem interested. How do I use the app?" Here is what professional guidance and research on augmentative and alternative communication suggest, and how to do each step in Sono. Every person is different, so treat this as a starting point to adapt together with a speech and language therapist, not as a programme to follow to the letter.

On this page

  1. Interest comes after exposure, not before
  2. Model: use Sono yourself while you talk
  3. Start with what they love
  4. Wait, then answer every attempt
  5. Keep it within reach, all day
  6. Every person is different: work with a professional

Interest comes after exposure, not before # — Link to this section

Children learn to talk after hearing speech around them for a year or more, long before anyone expects a word back. Someone learning to communicate with symbols needs the same: to see other people use their symbols, many times a day, in real situations. "Not interested" at the start usually means "hasn't seen it used enough yet".

Professional guidance is clear that there are no prerequisites for communicating with symbols. ASHA states that a person doesn't need to show any particular skill first, and the researchers who studied its use in early childhood call it a first line of help, not a last resort. Speech-language pathologists Cress and Marvin even suggest reading the sentence "this child isn't motivated to use the device" as a signal to look at the surroundings and at how partners respond, not as a verdict on the child.

What to do

  1. Decide that Sono is simply "on" every day for the next few weeks, like a language spoken in the home. Judge it after that, not after a few tries.
  2. Lower the bar to zero: for now, nothing is expected back. Your taps are the lesson.
  3. Count your own examples, not their taps. Ten short moments a day beat one long session.
  4. Expect a learning curve of weeks to months, and progress that isn't a straight line. In studies, understanding grew before the first independent taps.

  • AssistiveWare — Don't give up too soon
  • Drager et al. (2006) — The effect of aided language modeling on symbol comprehension and production in two preschoolers with autism. AJSLP 15(2)

Model: use Sono yourself while you talk # — Link to this section

Modelling, also called aided language input or aided language stimulation, is the strategy with the strongest research support for people starting to communicate with symbols. Reviews of dozens of studies found that children whose partners used the symbols while talking made clear gains in understanding and expression, across ages and ability levels. The partner talks normally and taps the key words on the person's system as they say them. That's all. The person isn't asked to copy.

How to do it in Sono

  1. Keep Sono open and within both your reach, in the layout the person uses.
  2. Say what you would normally say, and tap one or two key words as you say them. "Let's GO OUTSIDE!" Tap GO (Actions) and OUTSIDE (Places). "Uh-oh, the juice spilled. MORE juice?" Tap MORE (Basics). With the default tap behaviour, one tap speaks the word, which is exactly what modelling needs.
  3. Model one or two words at a time. Whole sentences come later.
  4. Don't test and don't quiz. Avoid "show me juice" or "say it on the tablet". Comment instead of asking.
  5. Model more than requests: comments (FUNNY, LOOK), feelings (HAPPY, TIRED) and refusing (NO, STOP, ALL DONE). Being able to say no matters, and it means more when it is respected whenever that is safe.
  6. In the Simple layout every tap speaks one word, which makes it the easiest layout for modelling single words. In Advanced and Menu, double-tap two words to put them on the sentence strip and tap Speak to hear them together: a good way to model two-word phrases once single words are familiar.
  7. Mistakes are fine. Tapping the wrong symbol and fixing it shows that everybody corrects themselves.

Start with what they love # — Link to this section

Nobody learns to ask for things they don't want. Clinicians who looked at children who "wouldn't use" their systems found that the words on offer often weren't motivating any more, or that the system was harder than the child's existing way of getting things. Guidance on choosing vocabulary says the same: words must fit the person's needs and interests and be useful in many situations.

How to do it in Sono

  1. Write down five to ten things the person asks for or enjoys: foods, toys, people, games like tickling or swinging, songs, a favourite show.
  2. Put real photos on the board. In Caretaker Settings > My Symbols, tap + and create a symbol with the person's own cup, toy or grandma: a photo from the gallery or the camera, a name and a word type. Your symbols appear on the My Symbols tab, the first tab in the Advanced and Simple layouts and a tile in the Menu layout.
  3. Replace a built-in picture with a familiar one under Caretaker Settings > Customise Symbols, and record a familiar voice for it there too.
  4. Hide what you don't need yet under Caretaker Settings > Hidden Symbols, so the board is calmer. Everything can be restored later.
  5. Keep the words that come up many times a day within reach: MORE, STOP, HELP, ALL DONE, AGAIN. On the sentence strip, the Quick button (the lightning bolt) speaks phrases such as More, Stop, Help and Wait with one tap; choose which ones under Caretaker Settings > Quick Phrases.
  6. Play games with a pause. Tickle, swing, blow BUBBLES, then stop, model MORE or AGAIN, wait, and continue the moment the person communicates in any way: a look, a reach, a sound, a tap.

Wait, then answer every attempt # — Link to this section

Studies of what makes partner behaviour effective keep finding the same three things: modelling, waiting expectantly, and responding to whatever the person does. Untrained partners tend to ask yes/no questions, fill every silence and miss the person's attempts. ASHA's guidance notes that an expectant look and a pause are often enough to prompt a response.

How to do it in Sono

  1. After you model, stop and wait. Count silently to ten. Many people need far longer to respond than we expect.
  2. Any response counts: a glance at the board, a reach, a sound, a tap on any symbol.
  3. Treat random-looking taps as meaningful. If the person taps STOP, say "Stop? Okay, I'll stop", and stop. This is how they learn that a symbol makes something happen.
  4. If they showed you something with a gesture or a sound and you understood, don't make them repeat it on the device. Communication is not a test.
  5. Don't correct. If they tap DOG for the cat, model CAT once, cheerfully, and move on.
  6. In the evening, open Caretaker Settings > Message History: it lists the last 20 sentences spoken from the strip, and the Recents tab on the board keeps the last 30 symbols tapped. They show which words the person is actually drawn to.

Keep it within reach, all day # — Link to this section

A communication tool only works when it's there. The Communication Bill of Rights, drawn up by a joint committee of US professional associations, lists the right to a working communication system at all times, and ISAAC puts it plainly: a person's communication system should never be taken away. ASHA adds that worries about screen time don't apply to a screen used as a voice.

How to do it in Sono

  1. Keep the device charged and with the person: on the table at meals, in the bag, at school, on visits.
  2. Don't remove it as a consequence, and don't save it for "practice time".
  3. Turn on Kiosk Mode under Caretaker Settings > Board Display so the screen stays awake while the board is open. On Android you can also pin the app to the screen; on an iPad, use Guided Access.
  4. Print a paper copy. Caretaker Settings > Print Boards makes a PDF of one category or of all of them, for the bath, the pool or a flat battery.
  5. Tell everyone around the person, grandparents, siblings, teachers and babysitters, the three to five words you're modelling this week, and show them where those words are.

Every person is different: work with a professional # — Link to this section

What helps one person may not help another. Age, motor skills, vision, hearing, how they process language, how they feel about communicating and what they enjoy all play a part. Professional bodies in the US, the UK, Canada and Australia agree that augmentative and alternative communication should start with an individual assessment and keep being adjusted as the person changes, and that the people around them need training as much as the person does.

A speech and language therapist (speech-language pathologist) can assess the person, help choose words and goals, and show you how to model. Many work with occupational therapists on how the person can best reach and tap. If the person already uses another communication system, don't switch to Sono without asking the people who set it up.

Ask for help sooner rather than later if

  • weeks of daily modelling bring no reaction of any kind,
  • the person seems frustrated or distressed by the board,
  • tapping is physically hard, or the pictures seem hard to see or tell apart.

Settings worth trying in the meantime: fewer columns or the Menu layout for bigger buttons, the Simple layout for one-tap use, Circle symbols, a recorded familiar voice, and real photos instead of drawings.
